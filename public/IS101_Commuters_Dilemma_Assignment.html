The Commuter's Dilemma

Objective

You have decided to purchase a 2026 Hyundai Tucson. Great choice — but which engine do you pick? The Tucson comes in three powertrain options, each with different fuel costs, maintenance requirements, and price tags. Your job is to figure out which version is the best overall value over four years of college.

You will use Google NotebookLM and external research to calculate the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) for all three versions, then make a data-backed recommendation based on your personal driving situation here in Las Vegas.

The Three Versions

Same vehicle. Same size. Same features. The only difference is what powers it:

⛽

Tucson ICE

ICE stands for Internal Combustion Engine — a traditional gas-powered engine. Lowest sticker price. Highest fuel cost. Standard maintenance (oil changes, transmission, etc.).

⛽🔋

Tucson Hybrid

Gas engine + electric motor. Better fuel economy than ICE. No plug-in charging needed — the battery recharges through braking.

🔌🔋

Tucson Plug-in Hybrid (PHEV)

Gas engine + larger battery. Can drive ~33 miles on electricity alone. Highest sticker price, but may qualify for federal tax credits.

The question is simple: does the fuel savings of the Hybrid or PHEV justify the higher purchase price compared to the basic ICE version? The answer depends on your commute, your electricity rate, gas prices, and how long you plan to own the vehicle.

What You Need to Calculate: 4-Year Total Cost of Ownership

The sticker price is just the beginning. Research and estimate the following for all three versions over four years:

Cost Category What to Research
Purchase Price (MSRP) Starting MSRP for comparable trim levels across all three versions. Use the same trim (e.g., SEL) for a fair comparison.
Federal & State Tax Credits The PHEV may qualify for up to $7,500 in federal EV tax credits. Check eligibility — the ICE and standard Hybrid do not qualify. Include any Nevada incentives.
Fuel Cost (4 years) Estimate your annual miles (e.g., 12,000). ICE: gas price ÷ MPG × miles × 4 years. Hybrid: same formula with better MPG. PHEV: electricity cost for short trips + gas cost for longer trips × 4 years.
Home Charging Setup (PHEV only) Level 2 charger + installation ($500–$2,000), or cost of using public charging. The standard Hybrid does not need to be plugged in.
Maintenance (4 years) ICE: oil changes, brake pads, transmission service, coolant, filters. Hybrid: similar but brakes last longer (regenerative braking). PHEV: same as Hybrid but may have additional battery-related service.
Insurance (4 years) Get estimates for all three. The PHEV may cost more to insure due to the larger battery and higher vehicle value.
Registration & Fees (4 years) Nevada charges an annual EV/PHEV registration fee in lieu of gas tax revenue. The ICE pays through gas taxes instead.
Depreciation / Resale Value What will each version be worth after 4 years? Hybrids and PHEVs may hold value differently than the ICE version.
4-Year Total Cost of Ownership All costs – tax credits – resale value = your true cost to own each version for 4 years
The key question: The Hybrid costs more than the ICE, and the PHEV costs more than the Hybrid. But each one saves you money on fuel. Does the fuel savings over 4 years make up for the higher purchase price? That is what your analysis should answer.
Step 1

Gather Your Sources

Find 4 to 5 high-quality sources that provide hard data on the 2026 Hyundai Tucson and the cost variables listed above.

Recommended sources:

  • Hyundai USA — Tucson page — MSRP, specs, and MPG for all three versions
  • Kelley Blue Book (kbb.com) — pricing, 5-year cost-to-own, depreciation data
  • fueleconomy.gov — side-by-side MPG and kWh comparison, annual fuel cost estimates
  • AAA Gas Prices — current Las Vegas gas prices
  • NV Energy rate schedules — your local electricity cost per kWh for PHEV charging
  • Federal Tax Credit Lookup — check whether the Tucson PHEV qualifies
Action: Upload your 4–5 sources (PDFs, downloaded articles, or pasted text) into a new notebook in Google NotebookLM.
Step 2

Use AI to Analyze the Data

Once your sources are loaded in NotebookLM, use the AI to help you make sense of the data. Try:

  • Ask for a side-by-side comparison — "Compare the 4-year cost of owning the Tucson ICE vs. Hybrid vs. PHEV based on my sources"
  • Extract specific numbers — "What is the MPG for each version?" or "What is the MSRP difference between the ICE and the PHEV?"
  • Calculate fuel costs — "If I drive 12,000 miles per year and gas costs $3.50/gallon, how much does each version cost to fuel per year?"
  • Generate a briefing document or FAQ to organize all the cost factors
  • Try an audio or video overview to hear the comparison presented as a discussion
  • Create flashcards or a quiz to test your understanding of the cost differences
Important: Do not take AI answers at face value. Click the citations and verify the numbers against your sources. AI can misread data, mix up versions, or estimate when it should be quoting. You are responsible for the accuracy of your final numbers.
Step 3

Post to the Threaded Discussion

Post a reply to the discussion board addressing the following:

Present your 4-year Total Cost of Ownership for all three versions of the 2026 Hyundai Tucson (ICE, Hybrid, PHEV). Show the key numbers: purchase price (after any tax credits), fuel or electricity cost over 4 years, estimated maintenance, insurance, and resale value. Paragraph 2: The Verdict

Which version is the best value over 4 years, and by how much? Does the Hybrid's fuel savings justify its higher price over the ICE? Does the PHEV's even higher price make sense after tax credits? Beyond cost, discuss practical factors: Do you have access to home charging for the PHEV? Is your daily commute short enough to run on electric-only mode? Would you actually buy this version? Why or why not?

Paragraph 3: The AI Tool Review

Give an honest review of Google NotebookLM as a research tool. Which features did you try? Which two formats were most helpful for analyzing cost data, and why? Tips for Success

  • Compare the same trim level — use SEL or a comparable trim across all three to keep the comparison fair.
  • Use Las Vegas data — local gas prices, NV Energy electricity rates, and Nevada registration fees make your analysis real.
  • Show your math — "The PHEV costs $4,200 more than the ICE, but saves $1,100/year on fuel, so it pays for itself in 3.8 years" is far more convincing than "the PHEV saves money on gas."
  • Calculate the break-even point — how many years until the fuel savings cover the higher purchase price? Is it within your 4-year ownership window?
  • Don't forget hidden costs — PHEV charging setup, EV registration fees, and potentially higher insurance can narrow the savings gap.
  • Verify every AI claim — click the citations, check the numbers. Your grade depends on your accuracy, not the AI's confidence.
