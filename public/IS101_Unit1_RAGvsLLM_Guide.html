RAG-Based Tools vs. General-Purpose LLMs for Academic Research

Not all AI tools are created equal for academic research. Some — like Google NotebookLM — restrict their answers to documents you provide and cite every claim back to a passage you can check. Others — like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini — draw on broad training data, which makes them flexible but also prone to fabricating sources. Neither one removes your job as the researcher: checking every claim.

Start here Meet the two tool types

Two very different engines are hiding under "AI chatbot." Knowing which one you're using changes how much you should trust it.

Source-grounded

RAG-Based Tools

e.g. Google NotebookLM. Retrieves passages from documents you upload, then answers only from those passages — with a clickable citation on every claim.

General-purpose

General-Purpose LLMs

e.g. ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini. Draws on broad training data to generate flexible answers — but with no source constraint and no reliable citations.

Recap How AI language models actually work

You met these two ideas in Watch & Learn — here's the fuller explanation, plus one more way to see it in action.

AI is probabilistic — not intelligent

Despite how impressive AI responses can appear, large language models are fundamentally word-prediction machines. When you type a prompt, the model does not understand your question the way a human would. Instead, it looks at the sequence of words you typed and calculates the probability of which word is most likely to come next, then the next, then the next — one word at a time.

Think of it like a very sophisticated autocomplete. When your phone suggests the next word in a text message, it's doing a simpler version of the same thing. An LLM does this at a massive scale — trained on billions of sentences, it has learned statistical patterns about which words tend to follow which other words in what contexts. It is matching patterns, not reasoning.

This means AI does not "know" facts. It has no memory, no beliefs, and no understanding. When it produces a correct answer, it's because the statistical patterns in its training data happen to lead to the right words in the right order. When it produces a wrong answer — or fabricates a source that doesn't exist — it's because that same word-prediction process generated plausible-sounding words that happen to be false. The model cannot tell the difference.

Non-determinism — the same prompt can produce different results

Because AI is probabilistic, it introduces randomness into its word selection. At each step, instead of always picking the single most likely next word, the model randomly samples from among the top candidates — a setting called temperature controls how much randomness. The practical consequence is non-determinism: the exact same prompt, typed twice, can produce two different answers. Both may be correct, both may be wrong, or one may be correct and the other fabricated.

Deep dive How source-grounded systems handle hallucinations

RAG reduces hallucinations — but does not eliminate them

RAG systems mitigate hallucinations by retrieving real passages from your documents before answering and attaching a citation to each claim. A 2025 study found that RAG with reliable information sources significantly reduced hallucination rates — with GPT-4 showing 0% hallucination when using a curated source, compared to 6% when using open web results (PubMed). Another framework, MEGA-RAG, demonstrated over 40% reduction in hallucination rates compared to baseline models (PMC).

The paradox: RAG can also increase hallucinations

A May 2025 study from Google Research found that while RAG generally improves performance, it paradoxically reduces the model's ability to abstain from answering when appropriate. When context is merely relevant but insufficient, models still try to answer.

Garbage in, garbage out

If you add a low-quality, biased, or factually wrong source, NotebookLM will faithfully cite the bad information. It will not fact-check your sources for you. The quality of your source collection directly determines the quality of the output.

Citation drift

The main failure mode occurs when a citation points to a passage that is related to the claim but doesn't actually support it. The citation number looks reassuring either way — only reading the passage tells you which case you have.

General-purpose LLMs: fabricated citations

General-purpose LLMs have a well-documented tendency to fabricate citations — generating plausible-looking references to papers, authors, and journals that do not exist. American University warns that "any submitted work referring to fabricated sources may constitute a violation, including material resulting from generative AI hallucinations" (American University).

Wrap-up Key takeaways

  • AI is probabilistic, not intelligent. It predicts the next word based on statistical patterns. Correct and fabricated answers come from the same process.
  • AI is non-deterministic. The same prompt can produce different answers each time. Every output must be independently verified.
  • RAG tools are not hallucination-free. They reduce unsupported claims, but roughly one in eight may still not be fully supported.
  • Citation exists does not mean citation supports. Always click through and verify.
  • Privacy depends on account type. Education accounts offer strong protections; personal accounts may involve human review.
  • You are always responsible for what you submit. Fabricated citations and unauthorized AI use are violations at most institutions.
